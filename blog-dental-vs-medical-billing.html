Dental Billing vs. Medical Billing: Key Differences Every Practice Should Know

They look similar from the outside — submit a claim, get paid. But dental and medical billing run on different code sets, different forms, and entirely different benefit rules. Knowing where they diverge is how practices stop leaving money on the table.

If your practice touches both dental and medical work, you're running two billing systems at once — different codes, forms, and coverage logic. Treat them the same and claims get denied, underpaid, or never submitted. Here's where the two worlds diverge, and where the overlap creates opportunity.

Different code sets: CDT vs. CPT and ICD-10

The most fundamental split is the language each claim is written in:

  • Dental uses CDT codes. The Current Dental Terminology set, maintained by the American Dental Association, describes procedures with "D" codes (D1110 for a cleaning, D2740 for a crown) — the exclusive procedure language for dental plans.
  • Medical uses CPT and HCPCS codes — describing clinical and surgical services across all of medicine, not just the mouth.
  • Medical also requires ICD-10 diagnosis codes. This is the piece dental teams most often miss: a medical claim must explain why a procedure was done. Dental claims are procedure-driven; medical claims live or die on a defensible diagnosis-to-procedure link.

When a dental procedure goes to a medical payer, you can't forward the D code — it must be re-expressed in CPT and paired with the right ICD-10 diagnosis, a step that's easy to get wrong.

Different claim forms: the ADA form vs. CMS-1500

The paperwork differs too, and the wrong form is an automatic rejection:

  • Dental claims use the ADA Dental Claim Form, built around tooth numbers, surfaces, and quadrants.
  • Medical claims use the CMS-1500 (facilities use the UB-04), organized around diagnosis pointers, place-of-service codes, modifiers, and provider fields the dental form has no equivalent for.

Cross-billing to medical means rebuilding the claim on a CMS-1500 — not reformatting the dental one. Many practices that "tried medical billing once" simply submitted the wrong form.

How dental plans work vs. medical plans

The coverage logic differs too. Dental benefits center on tight limits; medical on deductibles and medical necessity. Dental plans typically feature:

  • Annual maximums. Most dental plans cap total benefits per year at a low ceiling. Once the patient hits it, the plan pays nothing more until it resets.
  • Frequency limitations. Cleanings, exams, and X-rays are covered only so many times per period (e.g., two cleanings a year). Submit too soon and it's denied.
  • Waiting periods. Major services may not be covered until the patient has been enrolled for several months.
  • Coverage tiers. Preventive, basic, and major services are often reimbursed at different percentages (e.g., 100/80/50).

Medical plans revolve around a deductible the patient meets first, then coinsurance up to an out-of-pocket maximum — and coverage hinges on medical necessity documented by the diagnosis. There's usually no low annual cap, but a much higher bar for proving the service was required.

Medical-dental cross-coding: when dental bills to medical

This is where practices either capture significant revenue or lose it. Many "dental" procedures are, by nature, medical — and the medical plan is the correct payer:

  • Trauma. Repairing teeth or jaw structures damaged in an accident is typically medical.
  • Oral and maxillofacial surgery. Surgical extractions of impacted teeth, cyst and lesion removal, and similar procedures frequently qualify medically.
  • Sleep apnea appliances. Oral appliance therapy for diagnosed obstructive sleep apnea is a medical service, billed with the sleep diagnosis.
  • Biopsies and pathology. Submitting tissue for diagnosis is inherently medical, as are exams and imaging tied to trauma, infection, or a systemic condition.

The catch: each requires re-coding into CPT, an ICD-10 diagnosis, a CMS-1500, and often pre-authorization. Done right, medical benefits absorb costs that would otherwise burn through a thin dental maximum.

Coordination of benefits and money left on the table

When a patient has both coverages, coordination of benefits (COB) determines which plan pays first and how the secondary handles the rest. Billing medical first on a cross-codable procedure preserves the dental maximum for work only dental covers — stretching total benefits further.

So much revenue is left on the table for a simple reason: cross-billing is extra work most front desks aren't trained to do. The easy path is to send everything to dental, let the maximum run out, and bill the patient — or write it off. Procedures medical would have covered never get submitted there, leaving real, recoverable income unclaimed across a busy year.

What to look for in a biller who handles both

A biller fluent in only one side will quietly cost you on the other. Look for:

  • Genuine fluency in both CDT and CPT/ICD-10 coding.
  • A track record of medical-dental cross-coding, with specific examples (sleep appliances, surgical extractions, trauma).
  • Comfort with both the ADA form and the CMS-1500, including modifiers and diagnosis pointers.
  • Experience with coordination of benefits and sequencing claims across plans.
  • A handle on pre-authorizations and medical necessity documentation, which medical payers demand and dental rarely needs.
  • Clear reporting on what was cross-billed, collected, and where benefits were preserved.

Key takeaways

  • Dental uses CDT codes and the ADA form; medical uses CPT/HCPCS plus ICD-10 diagnoses on the CMS-1500.
  • Dental plans run on annual maximums, frequency limits, and waiting periods; medical plans on deductibles and medical necessity.
  • Many "dental" procedures — trauma, oral surgery, sleep apnea appliances, biopsies — are properly billed to medical via cross-coding.
  • Coordination of benefits can preserve the dental maximum and recover revenue most practices write off.
  • Choose a biller genuinely fluent in both systems — cross-coding, COB, and medical pre-authorizations.

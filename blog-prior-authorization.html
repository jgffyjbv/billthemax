Prior Authorizations: Why They Stall Payment and How to Speed Them Up

A prior authorization is a yes-or-no gate between your treatment plan and a paid claim. Get it wrong — or get it late — and even perfectly delivered care turns into a denial you can't appeal away.

What a prior authorization actually is

A prior authorization (PA) — sometimes called pre-authorization, pre-certification, or pre-approval — is a payer's requirement that you get written sign-off before delivering certain services. The plan is essentially saying: prove this care is medically necessary and covered under our criteria, and we'll commit to paying for it. Skip the PA, and the service is treated as if it never qualified for coverage in the first place.

Not every service needs one, but the list of those that do keeps growing. Services that commonly require prior authorization include:

  • Advanced imaging — MRI, CT, PET scans, and nuclear studies.
  • Many specialty and high-cost medications, especially injectables and biologics.
  • Elective and outpatient surgical procedures.
  • Durable medical equipment (DME) such as CPAP units, braces, and wheelchairs.
  • Inpatient admissions, sleep studies, and certain therapies (physical, occupational, behavioral health).
  • Out-of-network referrals and select diagnostic tests.

Requirements vary by payer, by plan, and even by the patient's specific benefit — so a procedure that needs no PA on one plan may absolutely require it on the next. That variability is exactly where delays begin.

Why prior authorizations delay care and payment

A PA introduces a mandatory pause between the decision to treat and the ability to bill for it. When that pause runs long, two things happen at once: the patient waits for care, and your revenue cycle stalls before a claim is ever submitted. The friction usually comes from a few predictable places:

  • Manual, payer-specific processes. Different portals, forms, and fax lines for every plan mean no single workflow covers them all.
  • Slow turnaround. Standard reviews can take several business days to a couple of weeks; expedited (urgent) requests are faster but must be justified.
  • Back-and-forth for more information. A payer request for additional clinical records can reset the clock and add days.
  • The whole claim hinges on it. Without an approved authorization number on the claim, the service auto-denies — no matter how clean the rest of the billing is.

Common reasons PAs get denied or stuck

Most stalled authorizations aren't true clinical rejections — they're paperwork problems. Knowing the usual failure points lets you head them off before submission:

  • Thin clinical documentation. Notes that don't clearly establish medical necessity against the payer's criteria are the number-one reason for delay.
  • Wrong or missing codes. A mismatch between the CPT/HCPCS procedure code, the diagnosis code, and the requested service triggers a stop.
  • Step-therapy and conservative-care rules. Many plans require documented failure of a lower-cost option (e.g., physical therapy before an MRI) before they'll approve.
  • Submitted to the wrong entity. Imaging, labs, and pharmacy benefits are often carved out to third-party benefit managers, not the medical plan.
  • Eligibility or timing errors. A lapsed policy, a retroactive plan change, or a request filed after the service was already rendered.

A step-by-step PA workflow

The practices that move fastest treat prior authorization as a defined process, not an ad-hoc scramble. Work through these in order:

  1. Verify eligibility and PA requirements up front

    The moment a service is ordered, confirm the patient's active coverage and check whether that specific plan requires authorization — and which entity handles it.

  2. Gather the clinical documentation

    Pull the notes, prior treatments, test results, and codes that prove medical necessity against the payer's published criteria before you submit anything.

  3. Submit through the correct channel

    File via the right portal, form, or benefit manager with complete, accurate information. Flag genuinely urgent cases for expedited review.

  4. Track every request to a decision

    Log each PA with its reference number, status, and follow-up date so nothing sits silently in a queue waiting on "someone."

  5. Respond to information requests immediately

    If the payer asks for more records, turn it around within a day — delays here reset the review timeline and push back care.

  6. Record the approval and attach it to the claim

    Capture the authorization number, the approved units or date span, and load it into your billing system so the claim goes out clean the first time.

Tips to speed approvals

You can't change a payer's review clock, but you can remove every excuse for them to slow down. The biggest levers:

  • Verify PA requirements the day a service is ordered — never after it's performed.
  • Build a documentation checklist for your highest-volume procedures so nothing is missing at submission.
  • Submit complete clinical evidence the first time instead of waiting to be asked.
  • Track every authorization in one place with owners and follow-up dates.
  • Use expedited review for genuinely urgent cases — and document why it qualifies.
  • When a PA is denied, appeal fast and request a peer-to-peer review.

Peer-to-peer reviews and fast appeals

When a request is denied on medical-necessity grounds, you usually have two fast lanes worth using before a formal written appeal. A peer-to-peer review is a direct conversation between your ordering provider and the payer's medical director — a chance to explain the clinical reasoning that a form couldn't capture. These calls are time-sensitive, so schedule them quickly and have the chart in front of you.

If the peer-to-peer doesn't overturn it, move immediately to a written appeal with additional supporting documentation. Speed matters at every step: payers set tight deadlines for appeals, and a missed window can close the door for good. The fastest-resolving practices treat a denial as the start of a clock, not the end of the road.

The real cost of a missed PA

When a required authorization is missing, the claim doesn't just pend — it auto-denies, and many payers won't grant authorization retroactively. The care was delivered, the staff time was spent, and the reimbursement is gone. Multiply one missed PA across a busy imaging or surgical schedule and it becomes a recurring write-off line that quietly drains the practice.

Key takeaways

  • A prior authorization is a payer's written sign-off required before certain services — without it, the claim auto-denies.
  • Imaging, specialty drugs, surgery, DME, and many therapies commonly require a PA, and rules vary by plan.
  • Most stuck PAs are paperwork problems: thin documentation, wrong codes, step-therapy rules, or the wrong submission channel.
